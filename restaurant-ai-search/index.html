What is AI search visibility for restaurants?

AI search visibility, sometimes called AIO or generative engine optimization, is the practice of making sure AI assistants can find, verify and accurately describe your restaurant when someone asks them where to eat. Unlike traditional search it does not produce a ranked list — the assistant names a handful of places in prose and stops. Inclusion appears to depend on having consistent information about your restaurant across several independent sources, specific attributes stated as text, and signals that the information is current. There is no paid placement inside an AI recommendation.

The shift is worth stating plainly because the numbers are unusual. Consumer use of generative AI for local business recommendations rose from 6% to 45% in a single year — the largest twelve-month behavioral change local search has recorded. ChatGPT alone was used by roughly 31% of consumers for business recommendations, and Google's AI Mode has passed a billion monthly users.

What makes this different from ordinary search is the shape of the output. Ten blue links plus ten more meant that being eleventh still produced a trickle. An assistant names three to five restaurants and stops. Being the sixth best-documented restaurant in your city returns nothing — the diner never learns you exist.

The corollary is that gains concentrate. A restaurant that becomes one of the named few captures a disproportionate share of a channel that is still growing fast, which is the entire argument for paying attention now rather than in two years.

The part most operators miss

You do not have to use ChatGPT to be affected by it. 82% of consumers read AI-generated review summaries — the automatic synopses now appearing above reviews on major platforms — and 23% say they would decide on that summary alone. Google replaced its Business Profile Q&A section with Ask Maps, where Gemini answers visitor questions about your restaurant automatically.

Which means a machine is already writing descriptions of your restaurant and showing them to people who have never opened an AI app in their lives. The question is not whether to participate. It is whether what it says is accurate.

Plumbing

Where each assistant actually gets its information

These systems do not share a source of truth, which is why a restaurant can be described accurately by one and not exist at all to another. Knowing which reads what tells you where a gap is.

ChatGPT

The open web, plus licensed partners

ChatGPT does not read your Google Business Profile. Independent testing indicates it leans on Bing-indexed web results together with its own selection logic, which means your own website carries far more weight here than your Google listing does.

It also now draws on licensed data. OpenAI signed an agreement with Yelp in 2026 that surfaces Yelp reviews, ratings, photos and business details inside ChatGPT — which quietly makes your Yelp listing part of your AI presence whether or not you think about Yelp.

Earlier analysis of ChatGPT's local results found business websites were the most common source type, ahead of mentions on other sites and directory listings. Your site is the asset.

Google AI Mode & Ask Maps

Google's own index and your profile

Google's AI features run on Google's core ranking systems and its own index, which includes your Business Profile, your reviews, and your website. This is the one surface where profile completeness translates fairly directly into AI answer quality.

Ask Maps in particular generates answers to visitor questions from exactly three sources: your profile fields, your reviews, and your site. If those disagree or are thin, it answers badly with your name attached.

Perplexity

Live retrieval with visible citations

Perplexity retrieves and cites sources openly, which makes it the easiest assistant to diagnose — you can see exactly which pages it pulled from. If a competitor is being cited and you are not, the citation list tells you which sources you are missing from.

Smaller share of consumer use than ChatGPT or Gemini, but disproportionately useful as a testing instrument.

Everyone else

Copilot, Claude, and the review summaries

Microsoft Copilot has its own reservation integrations. Claude and other assistants read the open web. And separately from all of them, the AI-generated review summaries on Google and other platforms are synthesizing your reviews into a paragraph that most consumers now read.

The practical implication of the whole picture: you cannot optimize per-platform. What works across all of them is having accurate, specific, corroborated information in public places. That is one job, not five.

Does ChatGPT use my Google Business Profile?

Not directly, in the way Google's own AI features can. Testing indicates ChatGPT relies on web results and its own selection logic rather than Google's local data, so your Google reviews and profile should not be treated as your whole AI strategy. It does read your website, and it now surfaces licensed Yelp content. The practical takeaway for a restaurant that has only ever maintained its Google listing is that it may be well represented in Google's AI answers and effectively invisible in ChatGPT's.

The honest section

What the research actually found, and what Google actually said

A great deal of confident writing about AI optimization is asserted rather than tested, and some of it is being sold as a service. Two sources have real standing here. Both are worth knowing before you buy anything.

The study that named the discipline

The term generative engine optimization comes from a November 2023 paper by researchers at Princeton, the Allen Institute, Georgia Tech and IIT Delhi. It did something unusual for this field: it tested nine tactics against each other under controlled conditions and measured which ones changed citation rates.

The findings were narrower than the marketing around them. What measurably increased the chance of being cited was including statistics with a clear source, quoting identifiable experts, citing sources explicitly, and writing in fluent, readable language. Each produced a lift in the region of a quarter more visibility.

What did not work: keyword stuffing, padding text, and artificially simplifying content without adding substance. Those moved nothing.

Two caveats the marketing literature tends to skip. The same intervention can lift citations on one engine and barely move them on another, because the systems differ underneath. And the gains, while real, are bounded — no strategy in the study doubled citation rates. This is a discipline of compounding small wins, not a trick.

Google's official position

On 15 May 2026, Google published its first consolidated guide, “Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google Search,” under a new Generative AI fundamentals section of Search Central. It is the clearest on-record statement about what works inside AI Overviews and AI Mode.

Its central framing is blunt: optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and is therefore still SEO. Google acknowledges that practitioners use the labels AEO and GEO, then argues the underlying work is unchanged, because its AI features run on the same core ranking systems as ordinary search.

Then it names six tactics you can ignore. Several of them are actively being sold to restaurants right now.

What Google says you can ignore

Not used

llms.txt files and other “special” markup

Google states you do not need to create machine-readable AI text files, special markup or Markdown versions of pages to appear in Google Search including its generative features, because Google Search does not use them. Its crawler may discover the file and treat it like any other text file. Google's updated wording is that keeping one is completely fine for other services that do use them — it simply neither helps nor harms you in Google. Worth knowing because several tools now sell auto-generated llms.txt as a feature.

Unnecessary

Chunking your content into fragments

There is no requirement to break content into small pieces for AI systems. Google says its systems understand multi-topic pages and can extract the relevant passage without the author pre-fragmenting the article.

Unnecessary

Rewriting content specifically for AI

AI features understand synonyms and general meaning, so rewriting pages to capture every long-tail phrasing variation is not required. For a restaurant this is genuinely freeing: describe your food the way you would to a guest.

Unnecessary

Special AI-specific schema

Ordinary structured data remains useful. Inventing AI-flavored markup on top of it is not a thing Google uses. Restaurant, Menu and FAQPage markup done properly is the whole assignment.

Counterproductive

Pursuing inauthentic mentions

Manufactured citations and placed mentions are called out directly. This matters for restaurants because “we will get you mentioned across hundreds of sites” is a common pitch, and the low-quality version of it does nothing at best.

Do this

Ordinary, well-executed SEO

Crawlable, fast, trustworthy pages. Content a person would find genuinely satisfying. Accurate structured data. Google's own summary of the guidance is that matching the fundamentals is what makes you eligible — and being genuinely worth citing is what wins the slot.

Do I need an llms.txt file for my restaurant website?

Not for Google. Google's guidance states explicitly that Google Search, including its generative AI features, does not use llms.txt files, and that having one neither helps nor harms your visibility there. Google also notes it is perfectly fine to maintain one for other services or systems that do read them, and some agent-oriented tools have adopted the convention. So: harmless, cheap, possibly useful elsewhere, and definitely not the thing standing between your restaurant and being recommended. If a vendor is charging you for one as an AI visibility service, that is the wrong thing to be paying for.

The actual work

What genuinely moves AI visibility for a restaurant

Ordered by return for a restaurant specifically. Note how much of it overlaps with things you would do anyway — that overlap is the point, and it means the effort is not speculative.

01

A menu published as readable text

The single largest determinant of what an assistant can say about you. A model building a recommendation needs quotable specifics — dishes, prices, dietary options. A restaurant with a readable menu supplies all of it. A restaurant with a photographed menu supplies a name and an address, which is not enough to recommend confidently over an alternative that can be described.

This is why the menu guide is the companion to this page rather than a separate topic.

02

Attributes stated in plain text

Real queries carry conditions: quiet, cheap, open late, good for groups, gluten free, has a patio, takes reservations, near a landmark. Matching requires each attribute to exist as text somewhere readable — on your site, in your profile fields, in your reviews.

A private room that seats eighteen and is never mentioned online does not exist to any of these systems. Write the sentence.

03

Corroboration across independent sources

A restaurant described consistently by its own site, its Google profile, its Yelp listing, a couple of directories and a local guide presents a coherent picture. One described differently in each place presents ambiguity, and ambiguity appears to suppress inclusion.

This is the mechanism behind the boring advice about consistent names, addresses and phone numbers. It was always about machine confidence; now the stakes are visible.

04

Recent reviews, and replies to them

Reviews are both a freshness signal and raw material. AI review summaries are generated directly from your review text, which means what your guests write becomes the description a machine gives of your restaurant. Recent reviews weigh more heavily than old ones.

Covered fully in the reviews guide.

05

Freshness on the pages that matter

Independent measurement puts a meaningful freshness premium on cited sources — AI systems lean toward material that has been updated recently. A menu page revised this season, current hours, a visible last-updated date. A site that has not changed since 2023 competes badly regardless of quality.

For a restaurant this is easy, because your menu genuinely changes. Just make sure the website changes with it.

06

Being mentioned by sources that get read

City guides, neighborhood roundups, local food writers, event coverage, relevant directories. Genuine third-party coverage is one of the few things the controlled research and Google's guidance agree on — and it is explicitly the authentic version, not a bulk citation package.

Being placed in a real vertical directory such as a local food directory does this job honestly.

07

Crawler access left open

Blocking GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot or Google-Extended removes any possibility of being named. The publisher logic for blocking them does not transfer: a news site loses revenue when its article is summarized; a restaurant sells tables, so being quoted accurately with your name attached sends people to your door.

Some site builders block these by default without telling you. Check your robots.txt directly — details below.

08

A site that is fast and structurally clear

Google's guidance notes that browser-based AI agents now analyze pages including their structure and rendering. A slow, script-heavy page that hides its content behind JavaScript fails both the agent and the human.

If your site is the obstacle, Search Converts builds fast restaurant websites designed to be read by both.

How do I get my restaurant recommended by ChatGPT?

There is no submission process and nothing to buy — inclusion is earned through documentation. In practice: publish your menu as readable text on your own site, state your attributes plainly (atmosphere, price range, dietary options, group capacity, hours), keep your details identical across your website, Google, Yelp and any directories that list you, collect recent reviews, make sure your site is crawlable by AI bots, and get mentioned by genuine local sources. Because ChatGPT reads the open web rather than your Google profile, your own website carries more weight here than your Google listing. Expect this to improve gradually as pages are recrawled rather than switching on at a date.

Technical, but simple

Check that you are not blocking the assistants

This takes two minutes and it is total when it goes wrong. Type your domain into a browser followed by /robots.txt — for example yourrestaurant.com/robots.txt — and read what comes back.

If you see any of the AI crawler names alongside Disallow: /, you are invisible to that assistant no matter what else you do. The names to look for are GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot (OpenAI), ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, Google-Extended, and Applebot-Extended.

Some website builders and security plugins block these by default, on the reasonable-sounding logic that AI companies should not train on your content for free. For a publisher that argument has weight. For a restaurant it is self-harm: you are not selling the text, you are selling the tables the text sends people to.

While you are in there, check for a stray noindex tag left over from when the site was built. It happens more often than you would think and it removes you from ordinary search too.

Should restaurants block AI crawlers?

No. Blocking them removes any possibility of being named in an AI recommendation, which is now the third most popular source of local business recommendations. The reasoning that leads news publishers to block these crawlers does not apply to a business that sells food rather than articles — being quoted accurately with your name attached is the entire outcome you want. Check your robots.txt directly rather than assuming, because some platforms block them by default.

A restaurant robots.txt, roughly

You do not need to list every crawler individually. A simple permissive file covers it:

# allow everything, including AI assistants User-agent: * Allow: / Sitemap: https://yourrestaurant.com/sitemap.xml

The only reason to name crawlers explicitly is if you previously blocked some and want to be certain the blocks are gone. If your file currently contains Disallow lines you do not recognize, that is worth having someone look at.

How to test whether AI knows you exist

Do this yourself, this week. It takes fifteen minutes and it is more informative than any dashboard.

  • Open ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity. Ask each the way a diner would: “best [your cuisine] in [your neighborhood]”. Note who gets named.
  • Ask a constraint question you should win: “somewhere with a patio that takes large groups in [your area]”.
  • Ask directly: “tell me about [your restaurant name]”. Check every fact it returns. Wrong hours and dead dishes are common.
  • On Perplexity, read the citations. They tell you which sources the answer was built from — and which ones you are missing from.
  • Repeat quarterly. Answers vary between runs, so treat one result as a signal rather than a verdict.
What arrived this month

When the assistant books the table itself

For about a year, AI agents could find your restaurant and then politely give up. On 10 August 2026 that gap closed, and it changes what “being recommended” is worth.

Through early 2026 the standard demonstration ended the same way: the model found the restaurant, showed the available times, and handed control back to the human. Reservations existed on the screen but not in the conversation.

Then OpenAI turned on restaurant reservations inside ChatGPT through OpenTable, Resy and Yelp. A diner describes what they want in ordinary language — neighborhood, time, party size, cuisine, budget, dietary needs, atmosphere — sees real available times in the response, and books without leaving the chat. Reservations run globally through OpenTable and across the US and Canada through Resy and Yelp, with Yelp also covering waitlists.

Google arrived at the same place from its own direction earlier in the year, adding AI-driven booking that finds a restaurant, checks availability and confirms the reservation as one flow. Microsoft Copilot has its own integration.

Why this raises the stakes on documentation

Previously a diner who got a recommendation still had to visit your site or your Maps listing to act, which gave you a second chance to convince them. Now the decision can complete inside the conversation.

That does two things. It makes being named in the recommendation itself far more valuable, because there is no second look. And it makes every attribute in the request — quiet, under $40, vegetarian, 7pm, party of four — a filter applied against documented facts. Undocumented facts do not participate.

Reservations were chosen as the first agentic use case deliberately: high volume, high intent, low stakes, because the worst outcome is an empty table rather than a disputed charge. Ordering is the obvious next step. When an assistant can place a takeout order the way it now holds a table, the restaurants with structured, machine-readable menus are the ones it will be able to order from.

What to do about it now

  • Be bookable somewhere an assistant can reach. If you take reservations only by phone, an assistant can recommend you and still not close the loop.
  • Waitlists count. Yelp's integration covers joining a waitlist, which matters for walk-in rooms that never took bookings.
  • Document the constraints people actually ask about. Noise level, price range, group capacity, dietary handling, patio, parking, late hours.
  • Keep the menu machine-readable. It is already the difference in recommendations and it will be the difference in ordering.
  • Nothing here is purchasable. Still no ad slot inside an AI recommendation. Documentation is the only route in.

Ordering under your own roof

Protect yourself

How to vet anyone selling you AI visibility

In June 2026 Google updated its guidance on hiring an SEO with questions aimed specifically at AI optimization pitches. They are good questions and they cost nothing to ask. Ask them of us too.

QUESTION 01

Does their advice cite official documentation?

A credible provider can point to a page on Google's own developer documentation behind each recommendation. If a pitch rests entirely on proprietary “AI ranking factors” that nobody outside the company can verify, treat it as opinion rather than fact.

QUESTION 02

Is it aligned with the May 2026 guidance?

Cross-check what is being sold against the tactics Google named as unnecessary. If someone is charging for llms.txt files, content chunking or special AI schema as their AI visibility service, they are selling work Google has said you do not need.

QUESTION 03

Do their tools align with that guidance?

No external tool has access to Google's internal ranking data. Dashboards showing an “AI visibility score” are estimates built on sampling. Useful directionally, not authoritative, and worth understanding as such before you make decisions from them.

Three more worth adding

  • What exactly will you deliver? Deliverables are checkable. “AI visibility” as an outcome is not.
  • Who owns the assets? Your Google profile, your website, your domain, your content. All of it should be in accounts you control.
  • What happens if we stop? A menu converted to structured pages stays converted. A rented citation package disappears.

Claims that should end the conversation

  • Guaranteed inclusion in ChatGPT or AI Overviews
  • A specific ranking position by a specific date
  • A “partnership” or paid placement with an AI company for recommendations
  • Bulk citation packages promising hundreds of mentions
  • Anything requiring you to hand over ownership rather than access

Nobody sells placement inside an AI recommendation, because nobody has it to sell. Anyone claiming otherwise has told you something useful about themselves.

Field notes

Where restaurants lose AI visibility

01

Treating Google as the whole strategy

A restaurant with an immaculate Google profile and a thin website can be well represented in Google's AI answers and effectively unknown to ChatGPT, which reads the open web instead.

02

Details that disagree across platforms

Different hours on Yelp, an old phone on a directory, a suite number that appears in one place and not another. Ambiguity reads as uncertainty, and uncertain businesses get left out of short lists.

03

Nothing quotable on the website

A full-screen photograph, the word “Welcome,” and a phone number. There is nothing here for an assistant to say about you, so it names a restaurant it can describe.

04

Blocking crawlers by accident

A default setting in a site builder or a security plugin, switched on by someone who never mentioned it. Silent, total, and takes two minutes to check.

05

Buying tactics Google named as unnecessary

Money spent on llms.txt generation, content chunking or special AI schema is money not spent on the menu conversion that would actually change what an assistant can say.

06

Never checking what is being said

Assistants confidently describe restaurants using stale data — dishes discontinued years ago, hours from a previous owner. Nobody tells you. You have to look.
