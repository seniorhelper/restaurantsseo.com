Restaurant reviews: the thresholds moved, and most restaurants have not noticed

In one year the share of diners who will only use a business rated 4.5 or higher nearly doubled, from 17% to 31%. Meanwhile 74% only weigh reviews from the last three months, which means a brilliant rating built on old reviews now reads as a restaurant that used to be good. This guide covers the arithmetic, how to ask without breaking federal rules, and what to write back.

How many Google reviews does a restaurant need?

At least twenty, with fifty or more as a working target. 47% of consumers will not use a business with fewer than twenty reviews, and only 9% will use one with five or fewer. But volume alone is not the goal — recency matters as much. Because 74% only weigh reviews from the last three months, you need roughly four new reviews a month, every month, to keep your newest review inside that window all year. Not a campaign. A habit.

The reassuring number in that table is the last one. 83% of people who were asked for a review wrote one, and the share who say they will “always” write one when asked rose from 16% to 28% in a year.

The bottleneck is not consumer reluctance. It is that most restaurants never ask, ask once and stop, or ask in a way that is easy to ignore. That is a solvable operational problem rather than a marketing one.

Volume is visible and builds slowly. Recency expires quietly. Almost every restaurant that plateaus is losing on the second one.

The trap

The recency problem that catches good restaurants

This is the failure mode that specifically punishes restaurants that were doing well. It is worth understanding because the symptom looks like nothing is wrong.

The pattern is consistent. You open. You are excellent. In eighteen months you collect two hundred reviews and settle at a 4.7. Then you get busy running a restaurant, and asking for reviews stops being anybody's job.

Three years later the number on your profile still says 4.7. Nothing looks broken. But your newest review is eight months old.

To a diner scanning quickly, that reads as a restaurant that used to be good — and given that 74% only weigh reviews from the last three months, most of what you earned has quietly stopped counting in their heads. To Google and to AI systems, stale reviews are a weak freshness signal, and freshness has been weighted more heavily since 2026.

The rating you worked for stops working for you, without ever going down.

Why four a month

The arithmetic is simple. The ninety-day window is about three months. To always have a review inside it, you need reviews arriving at least monthly, and you need enough margin that a slow month does not empty the window entirely.

Four a month gives you roughly twelve inside the window at any time, which reads as an active restaurant, survives a bad week, and compounds to nearly fifty a year. For most independents that is one review per two hundred covers or so — a genuinely modest ask.

The projector at the top of this page shows what happens at your current rate. If you are below four, the window is where you are losing, regardless of how good your rating is.

The other half: rating math

Ratings are averages, which means they get harder to move as volume grows. This is worth internalizing before you set a goal.

A restaurant at 4.2 with 40 reviews needs roughly 25 more at five stars to reach 4.5. The same restaurant at 4.2 with 300 reviews needs closer to 190.

Two consequences. Fix the underlying problem first, because adding volume to a broken experience just locks in the average. And if you are early, protect the rating aggressively — the reviews you collect in your first year set a floor that is expensive to raise later.

A habit that survives a busy Friday

  • One person owns it. Not everyone, which means nobody. A manager who asks two tables a shift.
  • Ask at the moment of genuine satisfaction — when a guest says something kind, not when the check lands.
  • Make it one tap. A short link or QR on the check presenter, replaced when it looks worn.
  • Check reviews at open and at close. Ten minutes twice a day beats a quarterly session.
  • Write the monthly count on the same board as covers, so it is visible when it slips.
Compliance

What you are not allowed to do, and why it now matters

Reviews stopped being purely a platform-policy question. There is a federal rule with real penalties attached, and it prohibits several things that reputation software still sells as features.

Not legal advice · verify with your own counsel

The FTC Consumer Review Rule

The Federal Trade Commission finalized its rule on consumer reviews and testimonials in 2024, and on 22 December 2025 took its first enforcement step, issuing warning letters to ten companies over potential violations. Civil penalties run to $53,088 per violation as of 2026, adjusted annually for inflation.

There is no small-business exemption. The rule applies to sole proprietors and single-location operators exactly as it applies to chains, and enforcement posture to date suggests each fake review is typically treated as a separate violation.

We are a search agency rather than a law firm, so treat this section as an orientation rather than advice. If any of it describes something you are currently doing, that is worth a conversation with an actual attorney.

PROHIBITED

Fake reviews, in every direction

Creating, buying, selling or publishing reviews from people who did not use your restaurant, or that misrepresent the reviewer's actual experience. This covers reviews you commission and reviews you post yourself.

Platform detection has also improved sharply — Google blocked hundreds of millions of policy-violating reviews in a single year, and Google, Amazon and the BBB have filed joint suits against review brokers establishing that buyers face consequences alongside sellers.

PROHIBITED

Incentives tied to sentiment

You may not condition compensation or any incentive on a reviewer expressing a particular sentiment. “Free dessert for a five-star review” is squarely inside what the rule prohibits.

The narrow version that can be permissible under the rule: an incentive offered for an honest review regardless of what it says, and disclosed in or near the review itself. Both conditions, not one.

PROHIBITED

Undisclosed insider reviews

Reviews written by owners, managers, staff or their immediate relatives without disclosing the relationship. This one catches well-meaning restaurants constantly — a new place opens, the family writes glowing reviews to get the ball rolling, and nobody thinks of it as deceptive.

It is, under the rule, and it is also easy for platforms to detect from shared devices and networks.

PROHIBITED

Suppressing negative reviews

The rule contains provisions on review suppression, including using unfounded legal threats or intimidation to remove honest negative feedback. It also addresses company-controlled review sites presented as independent.

Practically: you may flag a review that genuinely violates platform policy. You may not lean on a guest to take down an accurate account of a bad night.

The one most restaurants get wrong

Review gating

Gating is the practice of surveying guests first and routing only the happy ones to a public review link, while unhappy guests are diverted into a private feedback form. It has been sold as a feature by reputation management software for years, and plenty of restaurants are running it right now without realizing it is a problem.

It is prohibited by the major platforms' policies and it sits directly in the territory the FTC rule addresses, because the resulting public rating misrepresents the actual distribution of customer experience. Enforcement posture suggests a gating flow can count as a violation for each affected consumer.

If your review tool asks “how was your experience?” and then shows the Google link only to people who answered positively, that is gating. Ask everyone, send everyone to the same place.

Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

Only under narrow conditions, and platform rules may still prohibit what the federal rule permits. Under the FTC rule an incentive is problematic when it is conditioned on the reviewer expressing a particular sentiment — so “ten percent off for a five-star review” is out. An incentive offered for an honest review regardless of what it says, and disclosed in or near the review, is the permissible version. However, Yelp prohibits incentives in its review flow entirely, even disclosed and sentiment-neutral ones, and Google discourages transactional review schemes. Something legal under the FTC rule can still get your listing flagged. The safest and most effective approach is to simply ask, since 83% of people who are asked write one anyway.

The system

How to actually get reviews, without any of the above

Everything that works here is unglamorous and free. The constraint is consistency, which is why the goal is a routine rather than a push.

01

Ask in person, at the right moment

The single most effective method and the one restaurants use least. When a guest says something genuinely kind — about a dish, about the service, about the room — that is the moment. “That means a lot. If you have thirty seconds, a Google review would help us enormously.”

Not at the check. Not to every table. From the person they actually connected with.

02

Make it one tap

A short link or QR on the check presenter, a card, or a table tent. Google provides a direct review link from your profile dashboard — use that rather than sending people to search for you, because every extra step loses a share of them.

Replace the card when it starts looking worn. A greasy, faded QR reads as neglect.

03

Follow up if you have a channel

If you take reservations, run a waitlist, or have an email list, a short message the following day works well. Keep it to two sentences, link directly, and send it to everyone rather than filtering by predicted sentiment.

Once per visit. Restaurants that chase reviews with repeated reminders train people to ignore them.

04

Spread across platforms deliberately

The average diner consults about six sources. Google first, but Yelp, Apple Maps, Tripadvisor and OpenTable all matter, and Yelp content is now licensed into ChatGPT, which quietly makes it part of your AI presence.

A workable approach: default everyone to Google, and when a guest mentions they found you somewhere else, ask for that platform instead.

05

Give staff a reason to care

Reviews that name a server by name are the most persuasive kind, and they are also the most motivating for the person named. Read them out at pre-shift. Post them in the back.

Do not pay per review. It creates exactly the pressure that produces gating, fake accounts and staff soliciting from friends.

06

Fix the thing people complain about

If three reviews in a month mention the wait, no amount of asking will outrun it — you will just collect more reviews mentioning the wait. Reviews are a measurement instrument before they are a marketing channel.

The restaurants with the best ratings are usually not the best at asking. They are the ones with fewer things to complain about.

How do I get more Google reviews for my restaurant?

Ask, consistently, in person, at the moment a guest expresses genuine satisfaction — and make the act of writing one a single tap via a direct review link on the check presenter. 83% of people who are asked write a review, so the constraint is almost always that nobody is asking rather than that guests are unwilling. Give one manager per shift ownership of asking two tables, track the monthly count somewhere visible, and aim for roughly four a month to stay inside the ninety-day recency window. Do not incentivize by sentiment, do not filter who gets asked, and do not let staff write them.

The reply

Responding: write for the audience, not the reviewer

The person who wrote a one-star review has largely made up their mind. The fifty people who read your reply have not. That single reframe fixes most bad restaurant replies.

The numbers make the case for replying at all: 80% of consumers are more likely to use a business that answers all its reviews, 42% are unlikely to use one that never replies, and 19% now expect a same-day response — up from 6% a year earlier.

Partial response measurably underperforms full response. Answering only the positive ones, or only the negative ones, both score worse than answering everything. And templated replies actively hurt: 50% of consumers are put off by generic responses, so a canned paragraph under a detailed complaint reads as indifference.

One 2026 change worth knowing: review replies now pass through a moderation queue before publishing rather than appearing instantly. It usually clears quickly, so do not assume a reply failed to post.

The shape of a good reply to a bad review

  • Acknowledge the specific thing. Not “we're sorry you had a bad experience” but “a forty-minute wait on a Tuesday is not acceptable.”
  • Say what you are doing about it, if there is anything true to say. Vague promises to “do better” read as filler.
  • Offer to continue off the platform. A name and an email address, not a phone tree.
  • Stop there. Short replies read as confident. Long ones read as defensive.

And what not to do

  • Argue about the facts, even when you are right and can prove it
  • Mention what the guest ordered, when they came, or anything that identifies them
  • Imply they are lying, or that they are a competitor
  • Copy the same apology under fifteen different complaints
  • Reply while angry — write it, leave it an hour, then read it as a stranger would
★☆☆☆☆

“Waited 45 minutes for a table we had reserved, then another 30 for food. The tacos were good but we missed our movie. Won't be back.”

What not to write

“We're sorry to hear you had a less than perfect experience. We strive to provide excellent service to all our guests and we hope you'll give us another chance. Please contact us at your convenience.”

Addresses nothing specific, could be pasted under any complaint, and asks the guest to do the work of following up. This is the reply 50% of readers say puts them off.

What to write

“A 45-minute wait on a reservation is a failure on our part, and adding a 30-minute ticket time to it made a bad night worse. We overbooked the 7pm block that Saturday and have cut the number of reservations we take in that window. I'd genuinely like to make it right — I'm Maria, the GM, at maria@casaverde.com. Glad the tacos held up, at least.”

Names the specific failures, states a concrete change, gives a real person to contact, and stays short. The reader learns the restaurant noticed and fixed something.

How quickly should a restaurant respond to reviews?

Within the week at minimum — 81% of consumers expect a reply within seven days — and same-day is now expected by 19%, up sharply from 6% a year earlier. The practical version for a restaurant is ten minutes at open and ten at close, which handles nearly all volume without becoming a project. Batching twenty replies onto one afternoon every few months is visible to anyone reading the profile and reads exactly as what it is.

Damage control

Reviews you believe are unfair or fake

Every restaurant eventually gets one: a review from someone who never came in, a competitor, a former employee, or a guest describing an event that did not happen. Here is what can actually be done.

What can be removed

Platforms remove reviews that violate their content policies, not reviews that are merely negative or unfair. The categories that genuinely get removed are reasonably narrow:

  • Clearly not a customer — describing a business that is not yours, a location you do not have, or an event you can show did not occur
  • Conflict of interest — competitors, former staff, anyone with a demonstrable stake
  • Off-topic content — political statements, complaints about a neighboring business, rants unrelated to the dining experience
  • Prohibited content — harassment, slurs, personal information, threats
  • Spam or duplicates — the same review posted repeatedly, or obvious bot activity

What does not get removed: a genuine customer describing a genuinely bad night, however unrepresentative you feel it is, and however much detail they got wrong.

How to flag one properly

Flag it through the platform with a specific, factual reason tied to the policy it violates. “This is unfair” will not work. “This review describes a party of twelve on a date we were closed for a private event” might.

Reply publicly while you wait, calmly and briefly, because the reply is what future readers see regardless of the outcome. Something like: “We have no record of this visit and have asked Google to review it. If we have got that wrong, please contact me directly — I would like to understand what happened.”

What not to do: threaten legal action to force removal. Beyond rarely working, review suppression through unfounded legal threats is specifically addressed by the FTC rule.

The proportionate response

A single one-star review among fifty is statistically invisible and functionally harmless. The reply matters more than the review does.

What genuinely damages a restaurant is a pattern — five recent one-stars all mentioning the same thing. That is not a reputation problem to be managed, it is an operations problem showing up in public.

The best defense against an unfair review is volume of fair ones. A restaurant collecting four a month absorbs a bad one without anyone noticing. A restaurant that has not collected any since spring does not.

Two 2026 changes

Anonymous reviews. Platforms now permit reviews under pseudonyms in some cases, which raises volume and makes it harder to match a review to your own records. Reply to the substance rather than trying to work out who it was.

Reply moderation. Replies pass through a queue before publishing. If yours has not appeared, give it time before rewriting it.

The new stakes

Your reviews are now the raw material for AI descriptions of you

This is the development most operators have not yet absorbed. 82% of consumers read AI-generated review summaries — the automatic synopses that now appear above reviews on major platforms — and 23% say they would be happy to decide on that summary alone.

Those summaries are generated from your review text. Which means the sentences your guests write are being synthesized into the description a machine gives of your restaurant, and shown to people who will never scroll down to read the reviews themselves.

Google went further and replaced its Business Profile Q&A section with Ask Maps, where Gemini answers visitor questions automatically using your profile, your website and your reviews. You can no longer pre-write the answer to “is it loud in there?” — but your reviews are one of the three sources that answer gets built from.

What follows from that

  • Recent reviews carry disproportionate weight, because summaries lean on current sentiment. A themed complaint from eighteen months ago that you have since fixed can persist in a summary if nothing newer contradicts it.
  • Specific praise is more useful than generic praise. “Great place” gives a summary nothing. “The birria is the best I've had in Denver and the patio is quiet” supplies attributes that get matched against real queries.
  • Fixing an operational problem now has a compounding payoff — you stop generating the complaint, and newer reviews gradually displace it in what the machine says about you.
  • Your replies are part of the record too, and a pattern of specific, responsive replies reads well to both humans and systems summarizing them.

The full treatment of how assistants use this is in the AI search guide.

Do reviews affect whether AI recommends my restaurant?

They appear to, in two distinct ways. Reviews are a freshness and prominence signal, so a profile with recent activity looks like an operating business worth recommending. More directly, review text is raw material: AI review summaries are generated from it, and assistants building a recommendation quote attributes that reviewers mention. A restaurant whose recent reviews specifically name dishes, describe the atmosphere, and mention practical details gives an assistant far more to work with than one whose reviews all say “great food, will return.”
